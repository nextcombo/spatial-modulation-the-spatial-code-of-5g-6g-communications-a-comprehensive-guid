# Spatial Modulation: The "Spatial Code" of 5G/6G Communications - A Comprehensive Guide to Spatial Modulation Technology [with code examples]

In the field of wireless communication, "faster, more stable, and more efficient" has always been the core pursuit of technological evolution. From 4G to 5G, and then to the research and development of 6G, multi-input multi-output (MIMO) technology, with its parallel transmission capability, has become key to improving spectral efficiency. However, traditional MIMO faces challenges such as complex channel interference and heavy computational burden at the receiving end. The advent of "spatial modulation (SM)" technology, on the other hand, has opened a new window for wireless communication - it does not rely on simultaneous transmission by multiple antennas but allows the "antenna numbering" itself to become an information carrier, finding a delicate balance between efficiency and complexity.
I. Spatial Modulation: A Breakthrough from "Signal Carry" to "Spatial Coding"
To understand the value of spatial modulation, one must first return to the pain points of traditional multi-input multi-output technology.
The innovation of spatial modulation lies in its redefinition of "information carrier" - it no longer relies solely on symbols (such as QAM, BPSK) in the constellation diagram to convey information, but instead considers "which antenna is transmitting" as part of the information.
1. Core principle of spatial modulation: "Symbol + Antenna" dual carriers
Simply put, the workflow of spatial modulation can be decomposed into three steps:
• Information segmentation: divide the binary bit stream to be transmitted into two parts, one for selecting "active transmitting antennas" (e.g., 4 antennas need 2 bits to code antenna numbers: 00→1 antenna, 01→2 antennas, etc.), and another for selecting traditional modulation symbols (e.g., 16QAM needs 4 bits);
• Single antenna activation: at the same time, only allow one antenna to transmit signals while others remain "silent" (transmit power is 0), completely avoiding interference from simultaneous transmission by multiple antennas;
• Receiver detection: at the receiver end, use maximum ratio combining (MRRC) or maximum likelihood (ML) algorithms to first determine "which antenna is transmitting", then demodulate the corresponding symbol, ultimately stitching together the complete information.
1. Specific Example: When the system uses 4 transmit antennas + 16QAM modulation, traditional technology can only transmit 4 bits per moment (the number of bits in 16QAM), while spatial modulation can transmit "2 antenna coding bits + 4 symbol bits", totaling 6 bits, directly increasing spectrum efficiency by 50%.
2. The Emergence of Spatial Modulation: Addressing the "Efficiency - Complexity" Contrast of Traditional MIMO
The concept of spatial modulation was first proposed around 2006 (this article refers to an IEEE paper from 2008 as a key foundational document for this technology), its emergence was directly motivated by addressing the bottleneck of traditional MIMO:
• Traditional STC (Space-Time Coding) technology has strong interference resistance but low spectral efficiency (no matter how many antennas, each moment can only transmit 1 symbol);
• V-BLAST and other hierarchical technologies have high spectral efficiency but require the receiver to handle a large amount of interference, with complexity doubling with the number of antennas;
• Space modulation breaks this cycle: single-antenna activation avoids interference, and antenna number encoding enhances spectral efficiency, reducing complexity at the receiver.
2. Technological Combinations of Space Modulation: Ignite a Spark with Other Technologies
Spatial modulation is not an isolated technology; its combination with existing communication technologies further amplifies its advantages. Among them, the combination of spatial modulation + OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing) is the most classic and widely applied combination, also one of the candidate technologies for 5G.
1. Space Modulation + OFDM: Overcoming the "Frequency Selective Fading" Challenge
The core capability of OFDM is to split the "frequency selective fading channel" into multiple "flat fading subchannels", while spatial modulation solves the problem of multi-antenna interference. When combined, they achieve:
1. Enhanced Interference Resistance: OFDM mitigates frequency interference through subcarrier orthogonality and spatial modulation avoids inter-antenna interference via single-antenna activation, providing a dual safeguard that ensures signal stability in complex channels (e.g. indoor multipath and high-speed moving scenarios).
2. Frequency Efficiency Flexibility: By adjusting the "number of antennas" and "modulation order," the system can adapt flexibly to different channel conditions. For instance, during poor channel conditions, use "multiple antennas + low-order modulation" (e.g., 4 antennas + BPSK), and for good channel conditions, use "fewer antennas + high-order modulation" (e.g., 2 antennas + 64QAM), always maintaining optimal transmission efficiency.
The performance of this combination is verified in the referenced paper: under a spectral efficiency of 6 bit/s/Hz, SM-OFDM reduces the SNR requirement by 3dB compared to V-BLAST-OFDM at a BER of 10⁻³; in the presence of spatial correlation and antenna coupling among non-ideal channels, SM-OFDM's interference resistance is stronger than traditional technologies by more than 8dB.
2. Spatial Modulation and Channel Coding: Further reducing the Bit Error Rate (BER)
Spatial modulation can also be combined with convolutional encoding, Turbo encoding, and other channel coding techniques:
•
Encoding technology corrects transmission errors through "redundant bits," while spatial modulation itself reduces interference through single-antenna transmission. The combination of these two methods can further reduce the BER. Data from the paper shows that after adding convolutional encoding, the SM system requires a 5dB reduction in SNR compared to an unencoded system at a BER of 10⁻⁴, and still outperforms the V-BLAST encoded system by 7dB.
3. The Technological Advantage of Spatial Modulation: Why It Can Be a Candidate for 5G/6G? Compared to traditional multi-antenna technology, the advantages of spatial modulation can be summarized as "three highs and one low":
1. Higher spectral efficiency: The number of antennas is also "linearly increasing" the traditional MIMO spectral efficiency with the number of transmitting antennas, e.g., up to 8 antennas can transmit up to 8 symbols. However, the spectral efficiency of spatial modulation increases "logarithmically" with the number of antennas, requiring only 3 bits for encoding with 8 antennas and a total efficiency increase after adding symbol bits. Although the spectral efficiency of spatial modulation is slightly lower when there are an extremely large number of antennas, in practical scenarios (where terminal antennas typically range from 2-8), the efficiency of spatial modulation is sufficient and more flexible.
2. Low Receiver End Complexity: Significant reduction in terminal computational load. The complexity of the receiver end is a key factor determining whether a technology can be implemented. The complexity of traditional V-BLAST grows with the number of transmit antennas Nt in a "Nt³" manner (which requires matrix inversion). Iterative elimination). The complexity of spatial modulation only grows with "Nt×M" (M is the number of modulation levels). The paper shows that Under the 4x4 antenna configuration, SM's receiver complexity is 90% lower than V-BLAST. Equivalent to the low-complexity Alamouti technique. However, it offers superior performance.
3. Strong anti-jamming channel robustness: More stable in non-ideal scenarios., "Spatial correlation" (Multi-antenna signals are highly similar.), "Antenna coupling" (Inter-antenna electromagnetic interference), "Rice fade" (The existence of direct signal, such as channel distortion, etc., will severely affect the traditional MIMO Performance, while spatial modulation has higher tolerance for these non-ideal conditions due to single antenna activation.:   
•  
In the context of space-related, the error rate of traditional V-BLAST will surge tenfold, while SM only increases by 2-3 times.
•  
When antenna coupling exists, the SNR loss of SM is less than 5dB compared to V-BLAST and less than 1-2dB compared to Alamouti.
4. Flexible Antenna Configuration: Breakthroughs in "Receive Antennas ≥ Transmit Antennas" Restrictions
Traditional V-BLAST requirements that the number of receive antennas must be greater than or equal to the number of transmit antennas are being challenged by space modulation, which has no such restrictions — even when the number of receive antennas is less than the number of transmit antennas (e.g., a terminal with 2 antennas and a base station with 4 antennas), stable transmission can still be achieved. This is crucial for 5G scenarios where base stations have multiple antennas and terminals have few.

4. Future of Space Modulation: From 5G to 6G New Scenarios
Currently, space modulation has been included as an enhancement technology candidate in 5G NR, mainly used in indoor hotspots, industrial Internet of Things, etc.; in the development of 6G, there is even greater potential for space modulation:
• Smart Resonant Surface (RIS) integration: RIS optimizes channel through adjusting the phase of reflected signals, which can be combined with space modulation to replace "transmit antenna numbers" with "reflection unit numbers", further enhancing spectral efficiency;
• Optical Communication (VLC) integration: In optical communication, LED lights can serve as "transmit antennas", and space modulation can switch the activation state of different LED lights to convey information without complex simultaneous transmission;
• Satellite Communication integration: With limited number of satellite terminal antennas, the low complexity and high robustness of space modulation can meet the needs of satellite-ground communication.
Conclusion: The essence of space modulation is "using space for efficiency". From the "multiple antenna parallelism" of traditional MIMO to the "single antenna coding" of space modulation, it fundamentally represents a transition from "pursuing extreme parallelism" to "pursuing efficient balance" in communication technology. Space modulation does not pursue "more antennas, more complex algorithms", but through redefining "information carriers", it finds the optimal solution between efficiency, complexity, and robustness — this is precisely why it can occupy its place in 5G/6G.
In the future, with the development of antenna technology and AI detection algorithms, spatial modulation will continue to evolve, becoming a classic case of "using cleverness to solve big problems" in wireless communication.
